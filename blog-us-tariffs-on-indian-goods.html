US tariffs on Indian goods: what changed, what it means, what to ask your broker

Saldora Global · November 11, 2026

If you priced a shipment from India earlier this year and have not re-checked the duty picture since, your numbers are probably out of date. The US tariff stack on Indian goods changed three times in 2026 — not by a little, and not in the direction most headlines suggested. This post walks through what actually changed, what an importer of Indian goods pays today, and the questions worth putting to your customs broker before you commit to an order.

Tariff picture checked November 9, 2026 — see sources at the end. Tariffs change by proclamation and Federal Register notice, sometimes with days of notice, so confirm the position for your product and entry date before you rely on it.

What changed in 2026 — in order

The old additional duties ended. The tariffs imposed under emergency-powers authority (IEEPA) — including the reciprocal tariff on Indian goods and the separate additional 25% duty linked to Russian oil, which was removed for entries on or after February 7, 2026 — are gone. The Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs on February 20, 2026, and the 18% rate discussed in the February 2026 US–India framework announcement was never applied as a collected rate. If you are still modelling 18%, 25%, or 50% as the general additional layer for India, you are modelling a regime that no longer exists.

A temporary bridge followed. A 10% surcharge under Section 122 ran from February 24, 2026 and expired at its 150-day limit on July 24, 2026.

The current layer started July 24, 2026. Since that date, a Section 301 duty arising from the US Trade Representative's forced-labour investigations has applied to products of 60 economies. India is in the 10% tier — the tier for economies that ban forced-labour imports or have committed to do so — under HTS heading 9903.05.44.

There is still no signed India–US trade deal. As of October 12, 2026, the position on record was a framework for an interim agreement announced on February 6, 2026 — no signed agreement, no implemented tariff schedule from that framework. A framework is not a rate you can enter goods under.

What it means for an importer today

For most Indian goods, the duty picture today is built in layers:

  • The normal duty for your product — the Column 1 General rate for its Harmonized Tariff Schedule classification. This varies by product and is the layer your classification controls. Indian goods have had no Generalized System of Preferences (GSP) benefits since June 5, 2019, so the general rate applies unless the line itself is duty-free.
  • Plus the additional 10% Section 301 duty for products of India (heading 9903.05.44), on top of the normal rate, for goods not on the exemption lists.
  • Section 232 goods are treated separately. Goods already covered by Section 232 duties — for example certain steel, aluminium and other covered products — do not also pay the Section 301 layer above; they pay under the Section 232 programme instead. Which programme covers your product matters more than the headline rate.
  • Product-specific duties can still apply on top — for example antidumping or countervailing duties where an order covers your goods.

That is general guidance only, not a duty calculation for your shipment. Your total depends on your product's exact classification, its origin, any exemption, and its entry date — which is precisely why the next section exists.

What to ask your broker

Before you price the deal, not after the container sails:

  1. What is my confirmed HTS classification, and what is its normal rate? The classification drives everything else, and the importer of record — usually you — carries the legal duty of reasonable care for it, even when a broker files for you.
  2. Does the 10% Section 301 layer apply to my product, or is it exempt? Ask the broker to confirm heading 9903.05.44 against the current exemption lists for your line.
  3. Is my product under Section 232 instead? If so, the Section 301 layer should not be added on top — confirm which programme applies, in writing.
  4. Are there antidumping or countervailing duties on this product from India? These are product- and producer-specific and are easy to miss in a headline-rate estimate.
  5. What entry date are we modelling, and what changes if the shipment slips? In a year when the general layer changed in February and again in July, the entry date is part of the calculation.
  6. Did we pay IEEPA duties on earlier entries, and is any refund route open? CBP has run a claims process for IEEPA duties paid before they were struck down; whether an entry qualifies is an entry-by-entry question for your broker.

The short version

Forget the 18%, 25% and 50% headlines from earlier rounds. Today, most Indian goods pay their normal product rate plus an additional 10% Section 301 duty, Section 232 goods pay under that programme instead, exemptions exist, and there is no signed trade deal changing that yet. Classify first, confirm which layer applies to your line, and model the entry date you will actually hit.
