Shipping from Nhava Sheva to New York: an importer's checklist

Saldora Global · November 4, 2026

Nhava Sheva — the Jawaharlal Nehru Port, near Mumbai — is India's largest container gateway, and the port most US importers of Indian goods will meet first. A shipment on this lane rarely goes wrong at sea. It goes wrong on paper: a filing made a day late, an invoice that doesn't match the packing list, a classification nobody checked. This checklist walks the lane in the order the work actually happens.

Before you commit to the order

  • Know who the importer of record is. That party — usually you, the US buyer — carries the legal duty of "reasonable care" for classification, valuation and origin. Hiring a broker helps with the filings; it does not transfer the responsibility.
  • Get the goods classified early. Your product's Harmonized Tariff Schedule (HTS) number drives the duty picture and appears in the advance filing below (to at least six digits). Have your customs broker confirm the classification — and which additional duties currently apply to it — before you price the deal, not after the container sails.
  • Set up the US side. You will need an importer of record number and, for formal entries (generally commercial shipments over $2,500), a customs bond — a single-entry bond for a one-off shipment, or a continuous bond if you import regularly. If a broker will act for you, expect to sign a power of attorney.

Documents to get right at origin

  • Commercial invoice — specific product descriptions (not "samples" or "parts"), quantities, value and currency, buyer and seller, country of origin. This is the spine of the entry; customs builds its classification, valuation and origin view from it.
  • Packing list — contents, weights and carton counts that match the invoice exactly, and match what's actually in the container.
  • Bill of lading — check the draft before the vessel sails: shipper, consignee and notify party details are far easier to fix before departure than after.
  • Product-specific paperwork — some goods (food, textiles, anything regulated by another US agency) need extra documents or registrations. Ask early; these are the items that hold cargo.

The filing with the hard deadline: ISF

For ocean cargo, US Customs and Border Protection requires the Importer Security Filing — the "10+2" — before the goods are even loaded:

  • Eight of the ten importer data elements are due no later than 24 hours before the cargo is laden aboard the vessel at the foreign port — for this lane, at Nhava Sheva. The clock runs from loading, not from sailing.
  • The remaining two elements (container stuffing location and consolidator) follow as early as possible, and no later than 24 hours before the vessel arrives at a US port.
  • Late or inaccurate filings can draw liquidated damages of $5,000 per violation — against a deadline that is counted in hours at origin. Send your broker clean data early; do not let the ISF be the last thing anyone thinks about.

Arrival in New York / New Jersey

  • Entry and release. Your broker files the entry so customs can decide whether to release the goods, and should be ready before the vessel arrives — along with a plan for delivery once the cargo is released.
  • Exams happen. Customs may hold a shipment for examination. It is a normal part of importing, and the best preparation is the boring one: documents that agree with each other.
  • Entry summary and duties. Within 10 working days of release, the entry summary (CBP Form 7501) is filed with the final classification, value and duty calculation, and the estimated duties are deposited.
  • Keep the records. Importers must retain entry records — invoices, entry summaries, classification and valuation backup — for five years from the date of entry.

The short version

Classify early. Set up your importer number and bond before you need them. Make the invoice, packing list and bill of lading agree. File the ISF against the loading clock at Nhava Sheva, not the sailing date. And keep everything for five years.
